Scope creep has a reputation as something that happens in month three. It does not. It
happens in the first email, when both sides agree to a project neither has described, and
then spend three months discovering they meant different things.

These are the questions that surface the real project before a number gets attached to it.

## The eleven

1. **What happens if you do nothing?** If the answer is "not much", the project will lose
   its budget to something with a deadline.
2. **Who has to say yes?** Not who briefs me — who can stop this. Find the second name now
   rather than in week four.
3. **What has already been tried?** There is almost always a previous attempt. What killed
   it is the most useful information in the room.
4. **What does success look like in a number?** Not "a better experience". A number, a
   direction, and roughly by when.
5. **Who are the users, and when did you last speak to one?** The gap between those two
   answers predicts most of the difficulty.
6. **What is genuinely fixed?** Brand, platform, a date, an integration. Constraints are
   fine; discovering them late is not.
7. **Who builds it?** Design handed to a team that cannot build it is expensive theatre.
8. **What is the real deadline, and what is behind it?** A conference, a funding round, an
   arbitrary quarter. The reason tells you how movable it is.
9. **What are you not willing to change?** Usually more revealing than what they want
   changed.
10. **How will we decide when we disagree?** Agree the tiebreaker before there is a tie.
11. **What would make you regret hiring me?** People answer this honestly, and the answer
    is the thing to manage for the whole engagement.

## Ending conversations early is the point

Two or three of these questions regularly end a conversation before a proposal is written.
That reads like lost work. It is not. A project where nobody can name the decision-maker,
or where success has no number, is a project that ends badly for both sides after months
of effort.

The eleven questions take forty minutes. Finding out the same things in month three takes
the project.

## Write the answers down

Put them in the proposal, in the client's own words. Not as a contract clause — as a
shared record of what both of you believed at the start. When the scope shifts later, and
it will, the conversation is about a documented change rather than a disagreement about
memory.
